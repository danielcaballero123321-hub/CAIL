
This is a personal note, not part of the paper or its results. Nothing here is needed to understand or reproduce the work. I just wanted to write down what this project was like from my side. Feel free to ignore this completely.

Well, hello, my name is Daniel Caballero, I was born in Panama and I am 17 years old. 

Doing this project has been kind of a journey, really. It's been 9 months since I first started learning Python and here I am now, with a paper about ARC-AGI written under my name. It's a bit crazy to think that despite that I still don't know basic Matplotlib or other common Python libraries. I learned basic Python and went straight into PyTorch because I really wanted to do AI research. I even remember publishing a repo some months ago (on another account) where I shared the first things I built with PyTorch.  Those were simpler things, such as an MNIST neural network written in NumPy since I was trying to comprehend multivariable calculus that way or a ConvNeXt v1 replica for CIFAR on PyTorch. Other than the first months of this year, I have spent about five and a half months on ARC, since I started in June. Before CAIL, I tried this really weird idea where you had this pre-trained rule encoder that is trained with self-supervised frameworks such as JEPA or InfoNCE, and you basically gave it a demo pair from some task and then maximized (by evolutionary search for example) the similarity between the encoded vector of the demo pair and the encoding of a randomly generated answer passed through the pair encoder with the test input of the task alongside it. Weird, I know. I spent around 3 and a half months on that until I ended up discarding it when I discovered that improving the encoder performance on its self-supervised objective worsened the accuracy of its generations. I spent so much time on it because I was getting really close to solving an ARC evaluation task with it (like 2% missing). 

Although that didn't work out, it was so weird that trying to improve it gave me the experience and methods I would later use on CAIL. And more than the practices I developed because of it, I was so disgusted at the fact that I had to register each run manually on Obsidian or otherwise I would have to spend a lot of time searching for it across Colab notebooks, that I built my own app for it. 

It's called Nexus, and it is more a system than an app. The whole point is that every idea I try must be registered automatically, so, summarizing, it is a system that synchronizes with my Drive account, which synchronizes to my Colab notebooks through a library built for it, and then constantly recovers data from Drive to my PC. This includes training runs, custom benchmarks I design for my own models or anything I want, really. Once it is on my PC, the main app is a visual interface where I can see and compare with graphs whatever combination of runs I want over whatever metrics I need. This app is what allowed me to publish every one of the configurations used for the runs mentioned in the paper. Nexus does a lot of other things but I won't go into detail here since I plan on open-sourcing it on this GitHub account anyway. It will hopefully be published some days after this paper is sent to the ARC Prize Paper Track. 

Apart from all of that, the almost two months I have spent building CAIL were far less troublesome than the first project, mainly because I had almost finished building Nexus when I started CAIL so I managed to focus way more on what mattered. Not to say it was easy. Since I had no money whatsoever, I had to use a scholarship they give you here in Panama when you have good grades so I could fund my own research on CAIL.

I'm satisfied with the final results I got on CAIL, although I would definitely change a lot of things in my methodology, such as spending the budget better, since I spent like $25 of Colab compute on barely elaborated ideas. Now I get that when the compute is limited, the way you go about it is to first think of a hypothesis of something the model might be lacking or failing at, then you try to confirm it using specialized metrics or tests, and only then do you actually try and implement a solution for it. However, just checking if other ideas work on your setup isn't completely bad since that was how I discovered Muon works so well on CAIL.

Overall, the hardest part about all of this wasn't writing the paper or designing CAIL, it was doing all those things while I had to wake up at 4:30 a.m. to go to school and come back at 4:00 p.m. just to get homework anyway.

Apart from my own experience building this project, I wanted to write this note because I wanted to keep a public record of my own thoughts, since this is going to be seen when someone checks my GitHub when I apply to work at an AI lab, so I wanted to both keep track of the fact that I can recognize errors in my methodology, as well as remind myself of where I was before.
